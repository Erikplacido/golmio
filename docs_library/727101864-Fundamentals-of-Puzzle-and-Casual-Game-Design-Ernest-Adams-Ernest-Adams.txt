What Are Puzzle and Casual Games?
Puzzle games have been around for a long time, but they
really came into their own with the advent of mobile game
platforms and inexpensive, downloadable games. They’re
generally single-player omnipresent games that challenge
the mind rather than the reflexes.

Casual games are a market rather than a genre, games that
are easy for inexperienced players to pick up and play.
Casual games don’t require the player to devote too much
time to learning how to play, and are designed to work
with very short play sessions. They’re included in this e-
book because many puzzle games are also casual games.
Puzzle Games
Puzzles appear in games in several genres. Many single-
player computer games contain puzzles. In action games,
the player often has to figure out the boss opponent’s
weakness; adventure games are full of puzzles, frequently
about obtaining inaccessible objects or getting information
from other people; and even first-person shooters offer the
occasional puzzle, figuring out how to get past locked
doors and other obstacles. Puzzle design is an essential
element of game design, and it’s harder than you might
think.

In puzzle games, puzzle solving is the primary activity, though
puzzles may occur within a storyline or lead up to some larger
goal. That doesn’t mean that you can offer a random collection
of puzzles and call it a game; puzzle games usually provide
related challenges, variations on a theme, or a narrative. The
types of puzzles offered include recognizing patterns, making
logical deductions, or understanding a process. In all cases, the
puzzles give the player clues that have to be somehow
unraveled or solved to meet the victory condition.
To be a commercial success, a puzzle game needs to be
challenging (but not too difficult), visually attractive, and,
above all, enjoyable. It also needs to be fresh and to offer
enough gameplay to justify the purchase price. Although
solitaire card games such as FreeCell belong in the class of
puzzle games, unless you sell a lot of them together as a
collection, people are unlikely to want to pay for them.
Puzzle games require the player to change the game world
from an initial state to a solution state, usually by planning
ahead and manipulating objects in some way. Usually the
player can take as much time as she wants and can undo and
redo her moves, a quality that sets puzzle games apart from
action games, for example. A few puzzle games, such as
Bejeweled Blitz, have timers, but they don’t require the sort of
careful thought that a game like Sudoku does. If you include a
timer on a mobile device, you have to account for the
interruptions that occur, such as incoming phone calls, and
make the game easy to replay.
Because most puzzle games are abstract, the game world does
not simulate a real place, and usually the player does not enact
a role within the world. There are exceptions; in Sokoban, the
player takes the role of a warehouse custodian and has an
avatar, but her duties are limited to pushing crates around.
Unlike designing most other games, the process of designing a
puzzle game does not involve enabling some kind of fantasy
such as being an explorer or a race car driver. Many puzzle
games take place in 2D worlds with simple top-down views.

CLASSES OF PUZZLE GAMES
There are many kinds of puzzles in video games, and they
don’t all appeal to all kinds of players. If you want to design a
puzzle game, you’ll need to think about which of the various
types of puzzles you’d like to offer, and in particular the sort
of audience that you want to reach. Some options to consider:
  • Real-time or turn-based? Almost all noncomputerized
    puzzles, especially single-player ones, are turn-based and
    the player can think about them as long as she wants.
    Marble Drop is a single-player Maxis game about
    dropping marbles into a complex mechanism in such a
    way that they come out again in a certain order. Most of
    the puzzles are turn-based, but sometimes the mechanism
    has moving parts and the player must drop the marbles at
    the right time to produce the right result. Real-time games
    also tend to be time-limited (although Marble Drop’s real-
    time levels were not), placing the player under pressure.
    Time constraints allow you to control the difficulty level
    by play-testing the game to see how long it takes players
    to solve a puzzle and choosing an appropriate time limit.
    Puzzle-solving under severe time constraints—as in Tetris
    —belongs more to the action than to the puzzle game
    genre. Tetris and the many games similar to it depend
    more on physical coordination challenges than they do on
    logic problems.
  • Multiplayer or single-player? We tend to think of puzzle
    games as single-player, but they don’t have to be. Boggle
    is a real-time multiplayer game in which players have to
    find as many words as they can in a random grid of letters.
    Players earn points for finding words that no other player
    noticed.
• Randomized or premade? Puzzle games such as
 Minesweeper or Battleship use a simple mechanic and
 starting conditions that can be randomized every time the
 player plays. These games have minimal level design. To
 adjust the difficulty, you give the player more things to
 work on and perhaps fewer resources to work with,
 depending on what kind of a puzzle it is. However, you
 have to be careful that your algorithm for generating the
 initial state of the game world doesn’t produce an
 unwinnable situation—ideally the software should be able
 to detect these and reject them so that the player never
 sees one. (If the player does work herself into an
 unwinnable situation, she should be able to start over.)
 Alternatively, you can build a game with premade levels.
 This is much more work, but it enables you to introduce
 variety, and difficulty, into the gameplay in a well-
 organized progression.
• Combination locks. Many game puzzles are effectively
 combination locks. The player must manipulate one or
 more objects to achieve a final, unlocked state. The
 objects to be manipulated often have a hidden relationship
 to one another that the player must discover and take into
 account when manipulating them, just as the tumblers in a
 combination lock do. Rubik’s Cube is a classic
 combination lock puzzle; changing one thing changes a lot
 of other things as well. Most video game puzzles are not
 that complicated, however.
• Tile-matching puzzles. The player must make tiles match
 on a board in order to remove them. Bejeweled and the
 many, many other match-three games use this mechanism;
 so do Columns and Tetris, in different forms.
• Construction. Construction puzzles require building
 something in such a way that it is complete or operational.
 Usually the object can be built in a variety of ways, but
 most of them don’t work. Sometimes there is exactly one
 correct solution, but you can also create games that
 include a physics-based challenge, so any construction
 that meets the requirements is successful. World of Goo is
 an excellent construction puzzle game. The player has to
 build objects out of goo balls that meet certain
 requirements, but she may build them any way she likes as
 long as the object doesn’t fall apart. For further discussion
 of construction puzzles, see the sidebar “Case Study:
 Pettson’s Inventions,” later in this e-book.
• Navigation. These puzzles involve moving one or more
 characters through a space, overcoming obstacles along
 the way. The Portal games are a classic single-player
 example, set in a 3D environment; the Lemmings games
 require the player to manage a large number of semi-
 autonomous creatures and make sure that enough of them
 make it alive to the level exit to meet the victory
 condition. The Lost Vikings also required navigating
 characters through a 2D environment by controlling them
 one at a time.
• Hidden object/hidden word. Hidden object and hidden
 word puzzles show the player an environment—
 sometimes just a grid of letters—and ask her to find
 objects or words hidden in the environment. This isn’t a
 puzzle in the usual sense of being a brain-teaser, or
 requiring the player to plan a series of moves correctly, but
 people think of them as puzzles all the same.
• Brain training. These games usually include a variety of
 puzzles based on pattern recognition, manipulating letters
 and words, and doing arithmetic quickly. They are
 generally designed to exercise the player’s verbal and
 mathematical abilities, often with a time limit. Some also
 test the player’s physical skills.
• Lateral thinking and conceptual reasoning. These kinds
 of puzzles are normally found in adventure games. Each
 puzzle is unique because once the player has found the
 solution to a given challenge (such as using a vacuum
 cleaner to obtain something that is out of reach, for
 example), then she knows it and the challenge is no longer
 puzzling. Rather than using variations on a single
 mechanic, lateral thinking and conceptual reasoning
 puzzles require a different mechanic for each one.
 Adventure games are normally considered a different
 genre, because they involve so much storytelling,
 character development, and dialogue.
• Codes and ciphers. A fair number of adventure games
 include codes or ciphers, a class of puzzle in which the
 player must deduce the meaning of a message by finding
    clues elsewhere in the game, or working from internal
    evidence, such as knowing that the only one-letter words
    in English are a and I. These are fun for players to solve
    and require very little programming to implement. You
    can make the decoded message a clue in another puzzle,
    too.
  • Trivia. Trivia games aren’t puzzles in the traditional sense
    of find something or manipulate something into a desired
    end state, but they do test the player’s knowledge and so
    qualify as puzzle games.
These aren’t the only kinds, of course. Every now and then
someone creates a puzzle game that is completely unlike any
other, such as Katamari Damacy. Use your imagination! In the
next section we’ll look at how to design puzzles.

SCOTT KIM’S EIGHT STEPS
Scott Kim is a designer who creates puzzles for print media,
websites, and computer games. At the 1999 Game Developers’
Conference he gave a lecture entitled “The Art of Puzzle
Game Design,” in which he identified eight steps in puzzle
game design. The first four steps comprise the process of
specifying the rules, while the last four comprise the process
of building the puzzles and the game itself:
  1. Find inspiration. This can come from a variety of
    sources, including other games. Tetris, for example, was
    inspired by a noncomputer game called Pentominoes. You
    can be inspired by a piece of art (the drawings of M.C.
    Escher have a very puzzle-like feel), a story, or some
 particular subject matter. Another source of inspiration is a
 play dynamic of some kind: flipping switches, turning
 knobs, sliding objects around, or picking them up and
 putting them down. Or there are more complex dynamics
 among objects: balance, reflection, connection, and
 transmission.
2. Simplify. Suppose you have an idea for a puzzle:
 efficiently parking vehicles of different sizes in a crowded
 parking lot so that when someone asks you to retrieve his
 car, you have to move as few other cars as possible. Part
 of making this task fun is simplifying it to its essentials.
 First, identify the essential tricky core skill (in this case,
 space planning on the fly) and concentrate on that.
 Second, eliminate any irrelevant details. Don’t make your
 player worry about crashing the cars, for example. Third,
 make the pieces uniform. Instead of having cars with
 infinitely variable shapes and sizes, it’s better to have
 several standard types that conform to a square grid.
 Finally, simplify the controls. Figure out what the essential
 moves are and devise controls that implement them with a
 minimum of fiddling.
3. Create a construction set. The only way to be sure that a
 puzzle concept works is to play it, but obviously you don’t
 want to code up the whole game before you know whether
 it’s fun. You can build a paper prototype or a simple
 version in something like Macromedia Flash to see if it
 works. The rule designer can play with the prototype to
 tweak the rules, and later the level designer can use it to
  build levels. You can also code a construction set into the
  final game so that players can build puzzles for each other.
4. Define the rules. This is the key part of puzzle design.
  Most puzzles are characterized in terms of four things: the
  board (Is it a grid? A network? Is it irregular? Or is there
  no board at all?), the pieces (How are they shaped? What
  pictures are on them? What other attributes do they have?
  Where do they come from?), the moves (What is allowed
  and what is not? Are they sequential or simultaneous?
  What side effects do they have?), and the goal or victory
  condition (Does it have to be an exact match, or will a
  partial one do?).
5. Construct the puzzles. A puzzle challenges the player to
  get from a problem to a solution, but of course, the path
  isn’t simple. Every puzzle requires that the player make
  choices, some of which lead to dead ends. In an adventure
  game, each puzzle appears in a larger context (the story)
  that gives it meaning, and solving it advances the plot
  somehow. Some puzzle games also offer an overall plot of
  sorts or won’t let you try the next puzzle until you’ve
  completed the current one. Good puzzles require insight
  from the player, the “Aha!” moment that occurs when the
  player realizes how the puzzle works and how to solve it.
  But you mustn’t require an insight that’s too obscure, or it
  will feel unfair. If you tell the player that she’s in a maze,
  it’s unfair for the only solution to be to knock down the
  walls unless you indicate somehow that this is possible.
6. Test. Testing tells you several things. It tells you whether
  the puzzle is too easy or too hard (this can be difficult to
  predict in advance), and it also tells you whether it’s fun in
  the first place. It’s imperative that your puzzle be solvable,
  and if you haven’t already proven that it is during the
  construction phase, you definitely need to find that out in
  testing. Testing also helps you find out if there are
  alternative solutions that you didn’t think of, and it helps
  you discover errors in the rules. And, of course, it lets
  people try out the user interface. Because puzzle actions
  tend to be repetitive, it’s important that the interface be
  smooth and not frustrating.
7. Devise a sequence. Now it’s time to order all your
  puzzles into a sequence. The most obvious arrangement is
  a linear or accelerating sequence going from easy to
  difficult, but in practice, that becomes tiring and
  discouraging. A better arrangement is a sawtooth shape,
  which gets difficult for a while, then goes back to an easy
  puzzle, and so on, over and over. This creates an
  appropriate sense of pacing and lets the player have a rest
  before the puzzles become difficult again. And, of course,
  you can give the player the freedom to play the puzzles
  out of order or let her earn that right. You also need to
  think about transitions between puzzles, something that
  will keep her moving on to the next one. War games and
  role-playing games often do this with a storyline, but it
  doesn’t need to be that much work. Candy Crush Saga
  simply shows the player a path between levels. Or, the
  player can be working on a metapuzzle (a single large
  puzzle, parts of which the player solves in between the
  regular puzzles), which motivates her to complete the
  whole game.
8. Pay attention to presentation. Finally, of course, there
  are all the other details of game design: sound, graphical
  style, animation, user interface elements, storyline (if any),
  and so on. If you’re used to designing other kinds of
  games, it might be tempting to move this to an earlier
  point in the process, but with puzzle games, the puzzles
  are 90 percent of the battle. Get them right first, and the
  rest won’t be nearly as hard.
Case Study: Sokoban
    Sokoban is a classic, simple puzzle game devised back in 1981 by Hiroyuki
    Imabayashi. It still remains popular today and has been ported to many
    different machines. It’s particularly well-suited to touch screens.

    The Japanese term sōkoban means “warehouse keeper,” and that is the
    player’s role—pushing crates around in a warehouse to get them into their
    desired positions. It’s a 2D game based on a grid with an overhead view. The
    warehouse is a walled region, often with interior walls as well, and the walls
    are impassable. Each crate takes up one grid space and the avatar takes up a
    space. The object is to push crates into marked spaces—sometimes specific
    crates, sometimes any crate. Because the warehouse is crowded, the player
    usually has to move other crates out of the way in order to accomplish this.

    Looking at Scott Kim’s eight steps, you can clearly see how Sokoban, or any
    similar puzzle game, can be created.

    1. Find inspiration. Sokoban was obviously inspired by the challenges of shuffling
        goods around in a crowded warehouse, or perhaps the classic sliding-tile
       puzzle.
    2. Simplify. The challenge is simplified by using a rectangular grid and making all
        the crates the same size. The avatar occupies the same space as one crate
       and can move in only four directions. The weight of the crates is not a factor.
    3. Create a construction set. Any level of the game can be constructed out of four
        things: the walls, the avatar, the crates, and their target positions. Nothing else
       is required.
    4. Define the rules. The rules of Sokoban are particularly simple. The player can
       move one step in any of the four directions in each turn. In each move she can
       push exactly one crate in the direction that she is going. She cannot push more
       than one crate at a time. She cannot ever pull a crate, only push—to make it
       move in the opposite direction, she must go around and push from the opposite
       side. As a result, if a crate is in a corner it can never be moved again.
    5. Construct the puzzles. It is easy to lay out a Sokoban level, but harder to prove
        that it is solvable. The game is computationally complex. It may be necessary
       to establish the ending (victory) state and work your way backward to a starting
       position—bearing in mind that “backward” means pulling, not pushing, the
       crates back to their starting positions.
    6. Test. You should test your Sokoban levels both on yourself and on other people.
        If few people are able to solve your level, it’s too hard—and you should test on
       the general public, not just other game developers. Most Sokoban
       implementations keep a counter of the moves made, so if you have the game
       save this data you can see how long it took a player to solve a level.
    7. Devise a sequence. Sokoban levels are not related to each other, but obviously
       some are more difficult to solve than others. Good implementations order the
       sequence of puzzles according to the sawtooth difficulty curve recommended
       by Scott Kim, so that after a particularly difficult puzzle the next one is easier
       for a while.
8. Pay attention to presentation. Because Sokoban is so simple, it requires very
   few graphical elements. These should be attractive and clear, as in any other
   game. Many Sokoban implementations can be reskinned with a variety of
   graphics to suit the player’s own tastes, from photorealistic pictures of wooden
   crates and a man as the avatar, to purely abstract shapes.

WHAT COMPUTERS BRING TO
PUZZLES
Computers enable us to make a lot of puzzles that would be
impossible or expensive to create in the real world—consider
the logistics of supplying all the parts in Pettson’s Inventions
in physical form, including the cats and squirrels that appear in
the game. Even if a puzzle is physically possible, the computer
can add a number of useful features to make the gameplay
easier and more enjoyable.
  • Enable nonphysical or inconvenient moves. The
    computer can let players do things that don’t correspond to
    physical actions in the real world—for example, changing
    the color of something. You can also let the player control
    several things at once with just one key, something that
    would be inconvenient to do in a physical implementation.
  • Include computation features. You can use the
    computing power available to generate new puzzles
    automatically, find solutions to the current puzzle, or
    generate hints about what the player should do next.
  • Enforce the rules. In a lot of physical puzzles, it’s up to
    the player to enforce the rules. Sometimes players make
    mistakes and break the rules accidentally. A computer
    game can make sure that never happens.
  • Record player moves and allow the player to undo
    them. This very useful feature for games involves moving
    objects around in a sequence. The solitaire game FreeCell
 allows the player to undo one move, but it would be better
 if she could undo as many as she wanted, back to the
 beginning of the game. This rule is so important that I
 consider it imperative for long turn-based puzzles.
• Make it rewarding! It’s not unusual for puzzle games to
 have a funny or dramatic visual payoff when the puzzle is
 successfully completed.


Design Rule: Allow Players to Undo and Redo Long Turn-Based Puzzles
    Puzzles that require a great deal of backtracking and retrying can become
    tedious if the only way to backtrack is to start all over from the beginning. If
    the puzzle is complicated, the player usually cannot remember all the steps
    she had to take, and this creates unnecessary frustration. If this is the case,
    consider keeping track of the player’s moves, and allow her to undo and redo
    them as much as she wants.




• Structure the experience. The computer allows you to
 present the experience in a particular order, if necessary,
 passing automatically from one phase to another. In the
 real world, the player would be looking at the instructions
 and saying, “Let’s see, what am I supposed to do next?”
• Teach. You can include tutorial modes and step-by-step
 instructions to help your player get into the game. In
 puzzle games, these are seldom explicitly called tutorials,
 however; they are just the early levels of the game.
• Use bells and whistles. Obviously, with sound and
 animation, you can make a puzzle much more
 aesthetically interesting on the computer than it would be
 as a physical object.
• Enable online play. The computer lets players compete
 against one another, compare solutions, and be part of a
 puzzle-solving community. FoldIt (available at
 http://fold.it/portal) is a system that challenges players to
 solve real problems associated with how protein molecules
 are structured. They seem like ordinary puzzles, and the
 game gives points for solutions and provides a
 leaderboard, but the results have real scientific benefit as
 well. Eyewire is another such game with real-world
 applications. It’s available at https://eyewire.org/signup.
Case Study: Pettson’s Inventions
    Pettson’s Inventions is an imaginative and clever puzzle game for mobile
    devices that continues an idea begun many years ago in a similar game, The
    Incredible Machine. The game consists of a series of puzzles, each of which
    involves building a crazy machine to accomplish a certain task. The player
    constructs each machine in a two-dimensional space upon which a variety of
    mechanical devices can be placed. (Some of these devices are actually
    animals, which can be frightened by noises or lured by food.) Each puzzle
    starts with a few objects in given positions, and the victory condition states
    what the player must accomplish (for example, pop the balloon) using a
    limited number of additional devices. The player must place these devices in
    the play area and hook them together in such a way that the resulting
    machine, when set in motion, achieves the goal. Playing the game consists of
    adding elements to the machine, trying them out, adjusting them, trying them
    again, and so on. Figure 2 shows one of the completed scenarios in Pettson’s
    Inventions. Available parts are in the area at the left; this example shows a
    few leftover parts that are not needed.

    Scott Kim identifies three key design decisions that he feels made The
    Incredible Machine the prototypical construction puzzle game. These also
    apply to Pettson’s Inventions.

      • Allow the player to build things. This makes The Incredible Machine a
        construction game and differentiates it from, say, Tetris (an action puzzle game)
        or Marble Drop (a logic game in which the player decides where and when to
        drop marbles into a mechanism). The player is exercising his creativity.
        (Although Minecraft offers construction, it is a sandbox game, not a puzzle
        game, because it has no victory condition.)
      • Include no real-time decision-making. Constructing the machine and running it
         take place in separate modes. The player can take as long as she wants to
        think about what she’s doing. This is in contrast with Lemmings, an excellent
        game but one that requires the player to solve its puzzles on the fly. Often if the
        player doesn’t solve the puzzle in time, she has to start over.
      • Allow players to design their own puzzles. Little Big Planet is another great
        example of this. Any time players can build their own elements, it adds value to
        the game and helps create a community of devoted fans. Players can
        exchange their puzzles by e-mail, post them on websites, run tournaments, and
        enjoy all kinds of other activities, all of which constitute free publicity for the
        game.

CHECKING THE VICTORY
CONDITION
Bear in mind that players don’t always find the solution to a
puzzle the way that you envisioned when you invented it.
There might be more than one path to the goal. When your
game checks to see whether the player solved the puzzle, you
should test only to see whether the player met the victory
condition you gave her, not that she has done it in the way you
expected. Otherwise you’ve cheated her, and she’ll be
justifiably frustrated. She’s managed to get to the correct
solution state, but your game refuses to recognize it.
This problem appears in the game Interstate ’76, in which
players found an innovative solution to a problem, but the
game did not give them credit for it because it wasn’t the
“right” solution—even though the outcome was the same.
When the software checks the victory condition in your game,
be sure it checks the solution state that you told the player to
achieve, not the way in which she achieved it. Many games
also provide multiple levels of scores for different solutions,
which rewards players for partial success and encourages them
to keep trying for the best solution. It also makes the game
available to a wider audience because even players who can’t
find the ideal solution can still advance in the game. In Angry
Birds you must destroy all the pigs on each level, but you get
more points for doing it efficiently, and it rewards you with
one, two, or three stars depending on your score.
Casual Games
Gaming used to be an expensive and time-consuming
hobby, and it still can be, but it no longer has to be. Casual
games allow people who have little discretionary time (or
money) to still enjoy playing games during short breaks.
Casual games are not a single genre, because they can offer
many different kinds of gameplay. Rather, they have a
number of features in common that make them easy to
pick up and play. In this section, we’ll look at what makes
a game casual, and what features you should include (and
leave out) to make your game appealing to the casual
player.

QUALITIES OF CASUAL GAMES
Because casual games can belong to any genre, it’s not
possible to generalize about their usual camera or interaction
models, their progression mechanics, and so on. Rather, casual
games are defined by a set of characteristics that make them
appealing to a broad range of players, regardless of what the
genre is.
Casual players play when they have the opportunity, often in
breaks in their schedule or to pass time while waiting for
something else. Some play at a regular time of day to relax,
often after an eight- to twelve-hour workday. They may not
think of themselves as gamers at all and don’t consider gaming
a hobby or something they work at. It’s just a pastime.
Nevertheless, they may spend significant amounts of money
on it. Games aimed at this market earn hundreds of millions of
dollars every year; they’re an important part of the business.
Although casual gamers only fit gaming around the rest of
their lives, it doesn’t necessarily mean that they don’t play
much. Some casual gamers play for many hours a week.
If you want to make a casual game, you need to include these
qualities:
  • Easy and intuitive controls. The controls of the game
    should be self-explanatory or require only a minimal
    tutorial to learn. A designer cannot assume the player has
    a particular gaming background or muscle memory to
    drive this intuition. This is one of the reasons that so many
    casual games are found on tablets and mobile devices:
    touching and dragging are simple operations, and a touch
    screen offers the ability for WYSIWYG controls.
  • Short play sessions. After easy and obvious controls, this
    is the second-most important feature of casual games. The
    player must be able to get into the game quickly, feel as if
    he has accomplished something in a short amount of time,
    and get out again if he needs to. The loading and set-up
    times should be as brief as possible. Casual players often
    play a round or two of a game like Solitaire or Bejeweled
    while waiting for a bus. The play sessions don’t always
    have to be as short as three minutes, but if a player is
    required to play continuously for 15 minutes or more,
    that’s too long. A mobile game needs to account for the
    possibility that the player may be interrupted by a phone
    call at any instant. Bear in mind that offering short play
 sessions does not necessarily mean offering a short game.
 You can tell a long story over the course of many short
 sessions.
• A shallow difficulty curve and low frustration levels.
 Casual games should be very easy to start and should stay
 fairly easy throughout. If the player has a hard time
 completing a level, he will probably abandon the game.
 This doesn’t mean that casual games must always be
 trivial, but they shouldn’t be overly frustrating.
 Remember, the player is probably playing to relax, so the
 focus should be on success rather than failure. Cooking
 Mama moved the player ahead even if he failed at a
 challenge. See the next item.
• Hints and tips. Don’t let the player get completely stuck;
 provide hints and tips to help him move forward. It’s OK
 to reward the player for not using them—hidden object
 games often do this—but by offering them, you prevent
 frustration and make the game accessible to more people.
 Many casual games offer hints, powerups, or extra time if
 the player seems to be stuck.
• Auto-save or save anywhere. If your game is long but
 can be played in short sessions, the player will need to
 save it frequently. You can either save the game
 automatically at frequent intervals or allow the player to
 save the game at any point, so he can stop and do
 something else. Auto-save keeps the game simple and
 means the player doesn’t have to think about it. However,
 if you’re going to use auto-save, you must avoid saving at
    times when the player might prefer to go back and reload
    an earlier save. Most casual games don’t have branching
    storylines or offer players complex situations in which
    they might want to keep more than one save around.
  • Uncomplicated mechanics. Another aspect of keeping
    casual games accessible and easy to learn is fairly simple
    and obvious mechanics. Casual games seldom have
    complex internal economies that they intend for the player
    to tune carefully to get right. They often simply give the
    player resources at intervals and let him buy things with
    them, which eventually earns more resources in a slow
    positive feedback loop. Casual free-to-play games
    generally have a bottleneck in this system somewhere that
    prevents the player from advancing quickly, but which he
    can pay real money or trade social connections to
    overcome.
In addition to the qualities described, many casual games
include some additional features that you may wish to
consider:
  • Randomized levels for unlimited play. Games like
    Candy Crush Saga have a sequence of standard level
    layouts (although the arrival of new candy is randomized)
    that the player must play through in sequence, and often
    must pay to gain access to. However, you can give the
    player great value by offering levels with completely
    randomized content. Creating randomized levels is tricky,
    however; you have to be sure the level-generation
    algorithm doesn’t create a trivial or an unwinnable level.
• Easy social play. If you’re going to offer multiplayer
 gameplay, as with everything else about casual gaming, it
 must be easy to get into and out of, and not require the
 player to plan ahead unless he wants to.
• A light tone. Most casual games use bright colors and
 their violence, if there is any, is cartoonish. They also use
 comedy to keep the tone light, with silly-looking
 characters and amusing animations (see Song of Swords in
 Figure 3). Few casual games try to create a dark, moody
 environment. It takes a while for a player to become
 immersed in an environment like that, so it’s not suitable
 for the kind of gameplay that they can dip into for a few
 minutes at a time. Grim environments don’t work as well
 on the small screen either, because the contrast between
 the game world and the player’s real surroundings is too
 great.
• Inexpensive. Casual games are almost always inexpensive
 or free to purchase, at least initially, due to the competition
 in that space. To reach the casual player, you need to keep
 the barrier to entry low. Many casual games are monetized
 through in-app purchases, ads, “full” versions, and other
 schemes. There isn’t room to discuss all the various ways
 to do this, but Fundamentals of Game Design, Third
 Edition discusses it in Chapter 6, “Making Money from
 Your Game.”

MID-CORE GAMES
In the last couple of years a new category of game has
emerged, responding to a need for a new kind of gamer, mid-
core. Like most such terms, the definition is fluid. In this
section, we’ll consider what mid-core generally seems to
mean.
Hardcore gaming is, for the most part, a young person’s hobby.
(Naturally, there are exceptions.) It requires large blocks of
leisure time to learn how to play the games and to play them to
completion. As hardcore gamers get older, have families, and
take on more responsibilities, they may make a transition to
mid-core gaming. They are still capable of understanding
complex games, but they can’t spend several hours at a time
on a game. They also can’t afford to devote a lot of time to
learning the intricacies of a new game.
At the opposite end of the scale, the skilled casual player may
find that he is ready for a bigger challenge, a game that
requires more attention than most casual games do. He still
likes to play in short sessions, but is no longer content with
very simple games like Bejeweled. He also may be willing to
spend more money on a given game if it offers the sort of
sophisticated experience that he’s looking for.
The mid-core game, then, is one that is easier to pick up and
play than a hardcore game. Its controls should still be intuitive,
but you can expect the player to be willing to learn more about
them. At the same time, however, it has hidden depths that
most casual games don’t offer. Although the game is easy to
start playing, it takes longer to master. It requires more
attention, more brainpower generally, and includes more
complex mechanics. It is a richer experience than a casual
game, but it is not as challenging to get into as a hardcore
game.
Like casual games, mid-core games must not waste the
player’s time. Short play sessions require frequent
checkpoints, auto-saving, or allowing the player to save the
game anywhere.
Apart from the growing player base itself, mobile platforms
with touch screens are another reason that mid-core games
have come into existence. These devices don’t have the CPU
power nor the storage space required for a high-end hardcore
game, but they have enough to deliver an intermediate level
experience. The touch screen makes the user interface easy to
learn, but it does not support complex external controllers with
their many buttons and joysticks.
Competition Modes
Like casual games, many mid-core games are single-player
games, but they can also be multiplayer cooperative or
competitive. If you want to implement these modes, however,
the player must be able to enter them quickly and easily. Mid-
core gamers don’t have the time to gather a diverse group of
players for a raid as in MMORPGs. Allow your players to join
a group instantly and anonymously, play for a little while, and
then drop out with no harm done to the experience of the other
players. (For this reason, team play is problematic.) Don’t
require voice chat, either; these kinds of games are often
played in public where people won’t want to be seen shouting
at their tablet.
Pricing Models
The mid-core gamer is generally more willing to spend money
than the purely casual gamer, but not a lot more, and not on an
ongoing basis. You can sell a mid-core game for more than a
casual game costs in an app store (say, $4.99 instead of $0.99),
but obviously not as much as a conventional retail game costs.
Freemium models and in-app purchases are OK, but
subscription-based models are not; the player will probably
feel that he is not going to devote enough time to any given
mid-core game to justify the expense of an ongoing
subscription.
Case Study: The Puzzle Quest Series
    Puzzle Quest and its successors, Puzzle Quest 2 and Marvel Puzzle Quest:
    Dark Reign, are excellent examples of mid-core games. They aren’t pure
    puzzle games, because they aren’t about achieving a solution state through
    planning, and chance plays a large role in them. The developer chose the
    name simply because the game uses a match-three mechanic that looks
    rather puzzle-like.

    Puzzle Quest and Puzzle Quest 2 (Figure 4) are both single-player role-
    playing games (RPGs) with a story and a series of quests in conventional
    role-playing game style. The game awards experience points for completing
    quests and winning battles. The player’s avatar earns gold and power as he
    levels up, and can buy weapons, armor, and magic items. The game has two
    main gameplay modes: exploration and combat. But it also includes a number
    of features that make it more accessible than traditional computer role-playing
    games (CRPGs). Most noticeably, combat is simulated as a turn-based
    match-three game played against a single opponent. Matching gems on the
    board does damage to the opponent, or harvests mana or action points that
    may be expended by wielding weapons or casting spells. There are five types
    of mana, designated by color, and different amounts are required to cast
    different spells. The mechanics are moderately complex, but the player can
    choose from at most five spells in any one battle, and may wield only one or
    two weapons at a time.
      Figure 4 Puzzle Quest 2’s combat mode. The avatar’s status
      is on the left and her opponent’s status is on the right.


Because it is turn-based, the player does not experience much pressure
between combat sessions, a quality that makes it suitable for the mid-core
player. More importantly, the avatar cannot die and lose progression. If the
player loses a battle, he simply retreats and can try again. Being defeated
does not incur any penalty at all, so although the player has lost time, he does
not have to spend more time recovering; he can try again immediately if he
wants to. The game also auto-saves frequently, although not in the middle of
a battle, so the player does not have to worry about losing anything he has
gained. It also frequently opens up teleporters that enable the avatar to move
to any other teleporter, including one in the town where the player can buy
weapons and armor, thus keeping the amount of backtracking to a minimum.
Summary
Puzzle games provide the player with moments or hours of
strategy and problem solving. You’ll need to provide a
game that combines high-quality presentation with well-
thought-out game mechanics and interaction. Players can
be very opinionated about puzzle games, but the
individuals who enjoy puzzle games are often also very
loyal.

Casual games are not a genre but a kind of game that is
designed to appeal to a particular market: the casual
player. These players want an experience they can get into
and out of quickly, that is easy to learn and easy to control,
and that doesn’t require a large commitment of either time
or money. These games are usually found on mobile
devices and don’t have to cost a lot to develop.

DESIGN PRACTICE CASE STUDY
Choose a puzzle game that you believe, from your own
experience of playing it, is an excellent example of the genre
(or use one your instructor assigns). It should be a solitaire
(single-player) game where puzzle solving is the primary
activity. Write a report documenting this simulation. Be sure to
cover at least the following areas:
  • Describe the gameplay and game mechanics. Describe the
    presentation of the game and interaction of the player.
    How long is a gameplay session?
  • Which of the kinds of puzzle games described in this e-
    book is it (such as tile matching or navigation), or does it
    belong to another category entirely? Is it real-time or turn-
    based, with randomized or premade levels?
  • If the game has a physical representation in the real world
    (solitaire card games, for example), what does the
    computer bring to the video game version? What rules or
    designs were changed for the new medium?
  • Does the game have levels or increased difficulty? Is it
    clear to the player how the game progression works? In
    your opinion, does the game ramp up too quickly or not
    quickly enough? How does the game adapt to the player’s
    ability?
  • Address the combination of puzzles: Is there more than
    one type of puzzle in the game? Is the mechanism that is
    used to combine the puzzles into a single game clear to the
    player and does it make sense?
  • Discuss whether the victory condition for any level or the
    overall game is clear to the player. How is progression
    indicated?
In your report, use screen shots to illustrate your points. End
the case study with suggestions for improvement or, if you feel
the game cannot be improved, suggestions for additional
features that might be fun to have in the game.
Alternatively, choose a puzzle game that you believe is
particularly bad. Do the same case study, explaining what is
wrong and how it could be improved.
A case study is neither a review nor a design document; it is an
analysis. You are not attempting to reverse-engineer the entire
game but simply to explain how it works in a general way.
Your instructor will tell you the desired scope of the
assignment; I recommend from five to twenty pages.

Fundamentals of Shooter Game Design discusses designing
for this huge and specialized market. It examines both the
frenetic deathmatch style of play and the stealthier, more
tactical approach.
Fundamentals of Action and Arcade Game Design is about the
earliest, and still most popular, genre of interactive
entertainment: action games. This genre may be divided into
numerous subgenres such as fighting games, platformers, and
others, which the chapter addresses in as much detail as there
is room for. It also looks at the most popular hybrid genre, the
action-adventure.

genre that has been part of gaming since the beginning:
strategy games, both real-time and turn-based.
Fundamentals of Role-Playing Game Design is about role-
playing games, a natural outgrowth of pencil and paper games
such as Dungeons & Dragons.
Fundamentals of Sports Game Design looks at sports games,

contest itself is designed by others; the trick is to map human
athletic activities onto a screen and control devices.
Fundamentals of Vehicle Simulation Design addresses vehicle
simulations: cars, planes, boats, and other, more exotic modes
of transportation such as tanks.
Fundamentals of Construction and Simulation Game Design is
about construction and management simulations in which the
player tries to build and maintain something—a city, a theme
park, a planet—within the limitations of an economic system.
Fundamentals of Adventure Game Design explores adventure
games, an old and unique genre of gaming that continues to
earn a great deal of critical attention by its strong storytelling
and its visual aesthetics.
Fundamentals of Puzzle and Casual Game Design examines
puzzle games and casual games in general.
